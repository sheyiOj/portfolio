1. Problem Definition

The need

Career switchers receive advice built for people already inside the field. Someone moving from nursing into UX does not need a UX career guide; they need to know how a nursing CV is read by a design hiring manager, which experience transfers, and how long the switch honestly took someone who made it. The people who know are switchers one step ahead, frequently Nigerians in the diaspora, and they are reachable only through unstructured DMs they have every reason to ignore.

2. Solution Approach

Proposition

A career switcher browses verified mentors indexed by the transition they made, picks a real open time, writes what they want to discuss, and pays. The mentor arrives already knowing the agenda. There is no messaging in the product, before or after.

Core features

FeatureWhat it doesResearch it answers
Transition-indexed profilesThe career switch is the loudest element on the profile and the primary search axis, not the current job title"Advice is too generic and not specific to my situation"
Manual mentor verificationEvery mentor submits LinkedIn or documentary evidence of the transition claimed; admin reviews before going live, 48h target; verified status shown to menteesTrust substitute in a market with no reviews — load-bearing, see §3
Discovery & rule-based matchingPersonalised shortlist scored on industry, target role and goal keywords, plus full roster and typeahead search. Never returns empty."I don't know where to look"
In-app schedulingMentor sets weekly availability in their own timezone; mentee sees real open slots in theirs; double-booking prevented in the databaseRemoves third-party dependency and the payment sequencing bug, see §3
Questions box at bookingMentee writes their agenda as part of booking; reaches the mentor by email and notification"I don't know what questions to even ask" + the social fear finding: no cold opener to compose
Paystack checkout in nairaLocal cards and transfers, priced in the mentee's own currencyMarket fit at the payment layer
Held earnings & refundsMentor earnings held 72h after a call; published refund policy, in-product cancellation, dispute route, admin review with real refundsThe only recourse channel in a product with no messaging

Business model & unit economics

Mentor sets their own rate. The platform takes 15%. Mentors are shown the commission while they price, and — as a change prompted by this survey — the survey price band alongside it, so a mentor doesn't price themselves out of the market their mentees actually live in.

The honest read: at the north star of 200 completed calls/week and a ₦7,500 average, gross commission is roughly ₦975,000/month before processing — a proof of thesis, not yet a business. The MVP exists to establish that switchers will pay for transition-matched mentorship at all. Price, volume and a second revenue line are the questions after that, not before.

Two architectural decisions worth stating.

Correctness lives in the database. A booking can only move between states the database allows, availability is replaced in a single transaction rather than a delete followed by an insert, and double booking is blocked by a constraint rather than a check in application code. With one person building and no QA team, the app is the layer most likely to be wrong, so the guarantees were put underneath it.

Money is a ledger, not a column. Every paid call writes a ledger entry recording gross, the 15% fee and mentor net, with its own lifecycle: pending → cleared after 72h → batched → paid. Refunds void the entry if it hasn't been paid out, and record a platform-absorbed loss if it has. Financial state is reconstructable.

Answering the research that contradicts the product

1:1 paid mentorship ranked last, 3/11. Communities ranked first, 10/11. Three reasons I built the least-popular option:

  • Stated preference for a free thing is not willingness to pay. The same respondents named cost as the top barrier to mentorship (5/11) and named "affordable 1:1 guidance" as their top true need (6/11). Read together, they want 1:1 and object to its price — the ranking measures appeal, not demand.
  • Communities have a cold-start problem a solo pre-launch product can't solve. A community with 11 people is worse than no community. A 1:1 marketplace works with exactly one mentor and one mentee, and produces revenue on the first transaction.
  • Communities don't monetise this audience. At the price sensitivity measured here, a free community is a cost centre with no path to the 15%.

The bet is not that the survey is wrong. It's that the ranked answer describes what people want handed to them, and the barrier answer describes what they'd actually buy.

7/11 wanted anonymity. v2 removed it. Anonymity and verification are mutually exclusive here: the mentee is paying a stranger upfront with no reviews, so a real, checked identity is the only trust asset available — a verified mentor can't be anonymous. What I did instead was address the underlying fear rather than the requested feature: the anonymity demand tracks with fear of being ignored, rejected, or seeming inexperienced — the thing people avoid is composing a cold message to a stranger and being judged for it. The questions box removes that: the mentee writes their agenda into a form, after paying, to someone contractually obliged to show up.

This remains the least comfortable call in the product and is listed as a live risk in §4.

Pricing

DecisionPosition
Who sets the priceThe mentor, per 30 and 60 minute session
CommissionFlat 15%, shown to the mentor while they set their rate rather than discovered later on a booking
GuidanceMentors are shown the survey band, ₦5,000–10,000 for 30 minutes, at the point of pricing
RefundsFull or nothing, never partial — no figure to negotiate. Free cancellation at 24h+ notice.
CurrencyNaira only. No conversion, no foreign pricing.

The guided band is a change this survey caused. Unguided mentor pricing in a market whose median fair price is ₦7,500 risks a roster priced for a market that does not exist.

Promotion

Transition stories as the content, on LinkedIn and TikTok, with the mentor profile as the call to action. The content asset and the product asset are the same thing: a specific person who made a specific switch — chosen over paid acquisition because there's no budget, and because the survey shows the audience already consumes career content on exactly these channels.

Distribution

Supply first, by hand.

  1. Recruit mentors individually on LinkedIn and X, selected because their transition is already public on their profile — slow and unscalable, and the only way to fill a verified roster from zero.
  2. Verify and publish a small roster with real transition coverage in two or three target fields, rather than thin coverage across many.
  3. Point content at those specific profiles, so demand arrives at a mentor who exists rather than an empty search result.
  4. Hand-walk the first ten mentees from discovery to payment, watched, before any broader push.

Distribution is the least developed part of this plan and the biggest execution risk after the pricing assumption.

Evidence to date

The survey is the only user data that exists. Qualitative signal from it:

  • The strongest recurring theme is not information, it's cost — the most-cited problems in the instrument.
  • One respondent, a civil engineering graduate working as a tech VA for three years, describes themselves as "trying to find a niche in tech" — the target user in their own words.
  • One respondent wrote in, unprompted, wanting "a supportive community of people that has surpassed the stage I'm in" — transition matching, described by someone who'd never seen the product.
  • 2 of 11 volunteered contact details for a follow-up call — low, and consistent with a survey that asked for nothing and offered nothing.

No mentor-side research was fielded. Every mentor assumption in this document, including willingness to be paid through a platform at 85% and willingness to hold availability without a right to decline, is untested. That's the largest hole in the research and the first thing I'd close.

The critical assumption

A price-sensitive career switcher will pay a stranger, upfront, with no prior contact and no reviews, on the strength of a verified profile alone.

The survey supports the appetite and quantifies the price. It cannot test the payment, because nobody was asked to make one. 8 of 11 saying "maybe, depends on the price" is the sound of an untested assumption.

First test: ten mentees, recruited by hand, walked from discovery to payment, observed. The measurement is how many reach the payment screen and stop, and the data that matters is what they say at that moment. No dashboard is needed to run it.

What I would do differently

I did not research the supply side. Eleven mentee-leaning responses and zero mentor conversations, in a two-sided marketplace whose harder side is supply. Every mentor assumption in this product is a guess.

I built chat before deciding what it was for. The two-message cap, the moderation and the accept step shipped and were then deleted. The contradiction that killed them was visible in my own PRD from day one — I'd written both "paying reopens chat" and "two free messages" and never reconciled them. Reading my own document adversarially would have cost an afternoon and saved weeks.

I treated scheduling as plumbing. It was the funnel. Using a third party meant I never examined the sequence, and a defect where a mentor could go unpaid because a mentee didn't return to type a date sat in the product for weeks. Anything that touches the order of payment is product work, not integration work.
